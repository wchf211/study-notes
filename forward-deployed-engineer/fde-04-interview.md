# FDE Interview Fundamentals (Module 6)

> The interview tests the same qualities the job demands: knowing when to ask before proposing, thinking through problems without a clean right answer, and explaining work in terms people depending on it can use.

## 1. How FDE Interviews Work

Strong technical preparation is essential: a coding round means practical AI systems skills; a technical deep dive means architectural decisions, evaluation methods, and observability strategies. It rewards depth from designing/operating production AI. The least-practiced rounds are **decomposition** (a vague enterprise problem, where the first question matters more than the first system proposed) + **customer simulation** (the interviewer plays an enterprise buyer and the candidate runs discovery).

Three evaluation dimensions:
- **Technical depth** — design/deploy production AI; a baseline shared with AI engineers; surfaces in coding + the deep dive; tested in a deployment context. RAG means how to build retrieval for a specific customer environment, chunking/embedding decisions fitting their data, evaluation for production. Agentic means how to instrument an agent, define guardrail boundaries, design observability a customer team can operate post-engagement. Abstract answers miss FDE framing.
- **Deployment thinking** — handle a problem without a clear specification. Customers describe a symptom/desired outcome/pre-decided solution. The test is a vague open-ended scenario, and interviewers watch whether the candidate asks clarifying questions before proposing a design. E.g., logistics wanting to reduce shipment delays → immediately describing a multi-agent architecture skips: is shipment data accessible, who can approve automated decisions, the current manual process, the cost of a wrong automated decision. Interviewers watch the candidate distinguish what was said vs. what is actually needed and reach a problem statement before committing.
- **Client-facing communication** — run discovery, uncover the real problem beneath the stated request, explain tradeoffs in terms a non-technical stakeholder can act on. A dedicated round. Neither SWE nor AI engineer loops have an equivalent. Customer simulation evaluation means: asking questions surfacing real constraints, avoiding proposing a design before the problem is understood, closing with a clear problem statement the customer would recognize as accurate. Most directly replicates the day-one job; the format most candidates have never practiced.

SWE vs. AI Engineer vs. FDE: Problem type — SWE well-defined with one correct answer; AI Engineer technical precision expected; FDE ambiguous enterprise scenario, reasoning is the signal. Coding focus — SWE algorithmic arrays/graphs/DP; AI Engineer applied RAG pipelines/agent workflows/evaluation; FDE applied + scenario-grounded data processing/API integration/SQL. System design — SWE scale/infrastructure; AI Engineer AI system architecture; FDE enterprise integration compliance/access controls/legacy systems. Unique rounds — SWE/AI none; FDE decomposition + customer simulation. Behavioral — SWE team collaboration/technical decisions; AI similar to SWE; FDE client ownership/production accountability/ambiguity handling. Communication testing — SWE/AI minimal; FDE a dedicated client simulation round.

The biggest structural gap for SWE/AI candidates is problem type: a defined problem vs. a vague prompt deliberately. Evaluation is on asking necessary questions, breaking into coherent subproblems, articulating tradeoffs before committing. The same behavior defines strong discovery.

Research before the interview — four areas (2–3 hours before the recruiter screen changes every subsequent round): the company's **deployment model** (how FDEs embed, industries/customer types in public case studies, what a completed engagement looks like — system design answers reflect those specific environments, not a generic deployment); the company's **AI product + FDE position** (what the FDE owns end-to-end, closeness to product/research team, how field observations feed the roadmap — design around the actual product/tools the FDE uses; designing around an unsupported architecture misses context); **published technical content** (engineering blogs, case studies, conference talks show technical attention — a company writing consistently on evaluation means the technical round focuses on evaluation methodology; public writing on enterprise security means compliance constraints in system design); **job postings** (the tech stack in requirements is the surface the FDE deploys against; customer types in responsibilities are the environments in system design/decomposition; language describing a successful FDE delivers what the company values).

## 2. The Four Interview Rounds

Loops are typically 4–6 rounds over 3–5 weeks (varies by company; some combine elements across fewer sessions). Four core round types: Decomposition, Customer simulation, Technical, Behavioral. Each has its own format/criteria/preparation.

**Decomposition (60 min)**: tests systematic work through an ambiguous problem. Identify what is actually asked and reach a clear problem statement before proposing how to address it. Prompt: an ambiguous enterprise problem, no defined scope / single correct answer. Examples: a healthcare network reduce two hours physicians spend daily on clinical documentation; financial services automate loan application review currently 3–4 days per file for underwriters; retail handle 10,000 daily support tickets the team can no longer process manually within an acceptable response window. Evaluate: clarifying questions before proposing, clean decomposition, distinguish root causes vs. symptoms, close with a clear problem statement.

60-minute structure: Clarifying questions 10 min (users, data availability, decision authority, success metrics) → Problem decomposition 15 min (break into concrete subproblems) → Constraints + prioritization 15 min (what shapes architecture, what to build first) → Tradeoff discussion 10 min (name alternatives + cost of each) → Present back 10 min (a clear problem statement + proposed direction). The clarifying phase carries the most weight: before a model/architecture mention, establish users, data exists/format, the current manual process, the accuracy/latency threshold good enough for production. This reveals enough clean data, what the system must do each step, the success bar pre-deployment. A current variant extends into agentic system design: after the breakdown, design an agent — name tools, define the orchestration pattern, describe production evaluation. Rejection signals: jumping to a model/architecture before the problem is understood; treating the description as fully defined; closing with a problem statement no more specific than the start.

**Customer simulation (45–60 min)**: live discovery with the interviewer as an enterprise customer (typically a VP Engineering or Head of Operations). The scenario varies: AI to improve a slow internal process, automation for high-volume repetitive work, or get more value from existing data. Evaluate: active listening, restraint before proposing, discovery questions surfacing real constraints, closing with a problem statement the customer would recognize as accurate. Among the most heavily weighted because it most directly replicates the day-one role. Structure: open without a proposal; first ask the customer to describe the current workflow (trigger, who handles, where it breaks in practice); work through four areas: Workflow + pain (current process, where it fails most visibly); Data + systems (what data exists, where it lives, who controls access); Constraints + authority (compliance/security requirements, who approves what the system does); Success definition (what a successful outcome looks like after six months). Then synthesis: summarize what was heard and present a refined problem statement. The session closes there. Rejection signals: proposing architecture in the first five minutes; agreeing without questioning constraints; not closing with a clear synthesized problem statement.

**Technical round**: typically 3–5 hours across two formats: a coding assessment + a technical deep dive (same session or separate). Coding: practical AI tasks — processing/cleaning a dataset for model ingestion; calling an LLM API handling streaming + errors; building a retrieval component chunking + embedding documents; implementing an evaluation script measuring output quality; building a small AI feature end-to-end within a defined scope. Some companies use a take-home: submit working code + a recorded walkthrough explaining design decisions. The walkthrough carries as much weight as the code (what was built, why each decision, failure modes). Deep dive: an AI system design problem worked through in conversation. The system type follows the problem: document retrieval, a fine-tuned extraction pipeline, a single-agent workflow, or multi-agent orchestration. A consistent approach: **Start with the problem** (before naming a model/framework, establish what the system must do, data available, the success threshold); **Match architecture to the problem** (see table); **Account for enterprise constraints** (data residency, compliance, access controls, legacy integration shape what can be built/where; designs ignoring constraints miss FDE framing); **Define evaluation before deployment** (name what good looks like, how it is measured, the acceptable production threshold — evaluation is a design decision, not an afterthought).

Problem → Architecture table: Q&A over a large internal document corpus → RAG pipeline retrieval + generation. Structured extraction from domain-specific documents with labeled data → a fine-tuned model on labeled examples. Multi-step workflow requiring tool use + conditional routing → single-agent or multi-agent orchestration. High-volume classification/routing → a structured LLM call with constrained output. Combination retrieval + agentic tool use → a hybrid RAG + agentic architecture. Architecture is a starting point; interviewers evaluate the reasoning behind the choice as much as the choice. Rejection signals: naming architecture before establishing what the system needs to do; designing without enterprise constraints; presenting evaluation as a footnote vs. a design decision.

**Behavioral**: a structured past-experience interview, with answers in **STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Unlike a standard engineering behavioral (technical judgment/team collaboration), FDE behavioral surfaces customer-facing judgment: behaved when a deployment was failing, a customer pushed back, scope expanded, or AI produced a wrong output in front of a stakeholder. Example questions: a time you stopped a project and communicated the decision to a customer; a deployment that went wrong — what happened, what you did; a time you pushed back on scope creep; a time you explained an AI failure to a non-technical executive. The interviewer is not asking what you built; they are asking how you handled it when something went wrong/difficult. Structure each STAR element centered on the customer + constraint: Situation (customer, engagement context, what made it difficult); Task (the constraint/problem needing resolving); Action (the judgment call made, not just executed; why that choice over alternatives); Result (what concretely changed for the customer). The Result carries the most weight. A technical milestone without a customer change does not land. The interviewer measures thinking in customer outcomes vs. engineering deliverables. Some companies add a values/culture dimension or a separate session. Evaluation is on engaging real tradeoffs vs. prepared answers. Rejection signals: stories where the customer is background, not center; results naming a technical milestone without a customer change; answers describing what happened without naming a decision + why.

All rounds test the same orientation from a different angle: the customer problem at the center of every decision/conversation/story.
